# Adaptive coaching: three worked examples

These demonstrations develop practical moves from Webb's Chapters 12, 13 and 18. **Source notes are short paraphrases of the book. All user inputs, dialogue, branches and handoff examples are original hypothetical adaptations.** They are not book quotations, actual conversations, evaluated model outputs or evidence of coaching effectiveness.

Read the relevant card first. Use one matching demonstration when a contrast, changed-information branch or stopping point would help. Follow the user's actual request and facts; never import a fictional response into their case. In live coaching, ask only the current useful question and wait. In conversation preparation, present a scaffold and conditional options. A normal answer need not reproduce the tables or the whole sequence.

## 1. Feedback: let the other account change the remedy

**Primary card:** [Factual Feedback](../cards/12-feedback.md), Chapter 12, pp. 180–193. Source anchors: Observation → Stick to the facts; Invitation; Collaboration.

**Source note.** Webb contrasts concrete deadline observations with character labels and guessed causes. Her hypothetical software scene shows how an invitation can reveal a capability gap and reluctance to ask for help, rather than the assumed time-management problem. The proposed response combines learning support with earlier reporting of difficulties. This is a teaching scenario, not a reported test of this skill.

### Situation and fit

**Hypothetical user:** “Two agreed handoffs arrived late. The receiving team postponed testing. I think the colleague doesn't care. Help me prepare a private conversation.”

The events and impact are user reports. “Doesn't care” is an interpretation; the colleague's account is unknown. The request already establishes the goal, so do not restart intake. Factual Feedback fits a specific behavior and its impact. A support or development method becomes relevant only if new information establishes a distinct need.

### Weaker and better moves on the same facts

**Weaker:** “Why don't you care about the deadlines?” The question presupposes a motive and makes agreement with the accusation the apparent goal.

**Better original scaffold:** “I want our handoffs to be reliable. The two agreed handoffs arrived late, and the receiving team postponed testing. How did the situation look from your side?”

Use the user's genuine purpose and verified examples. If the leader knows an event only secondhand, attribute the report rather than presenting it as their direct observation. Then listen before proposing a remedy.

### New information changes the next move

The following replies are fictional branches, not predictions:

| New account | Useful next move | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “I got stuck using the new tool and was embarrassed to ask.” | Reflect that account, identify the specific learning need and discuss accessible help. Agree how to raise a blocker earlier. [Chapter 13](../cards/13-develop.md) can support capability-building if needed. | Calling the problem laziness or assuming training alone will make future blockers visible. |
| “The other urgent assignment took the time we had reserved.” | Clarify the conflicting commitments and prepare a priority or capacity trade-off for the appropriate owner. | Repeating the same deadline demand or promising to change someone else's commitments. |
| “You changed the requirements after we agreed the handoff, and now you're blaming me.” | Treat this as a reported account to check. A possible reply is: “I want to understand what changed. What did I miss in the agreement or in my own contribution?” If the leader's contribution is supported, acknowledge it and revise the agreement together. | Treating the complaint as proof of defiance, denying an unexamined contribution, or inventing an apology or admission. |
| “I don't want to discuss the personal reasons.” | Respect that limit. A possible reply is: “You don't need to explain private circumstances. We can focus on what would make the next handoff workable.” If they decline the work discussion too, pause; identify what work agreement remains unresolved and consider the appropriate human process. | Extracting private details, treating silence as agreement, promising secrecy, or deciding an employment consequence. |

If the user later reports that the handoff agreement itself was ambiguous, revise the opening accordingly. Keep the earlier interpretation from surviving as an established fact after a correction.

### Stop, hand off and practise

For preparation, stop when the user has an accurate opening, a genuine invitation and a way to choose support after listening. For a reported completed conversation, move to a specific proposed agreement once the relevant account is available; further motive-probing may add nothing. A declined conversation or continuing personal attacks can justify a pause; do not invent a restart date, force disclosure or claim agreement. Do not manufacture a deadline, permission for training or a successful next handoff.

**Example handoff:** “Prepare the factual opening. The cause is still unknown. If the colleague describes a skill gap, discuss learning support and early blocker reporting; if they describe competing demands, clarify priorities with the relevant owner.” This is readiness for a conversation, not an achieved outcome. The assistant has not contacted the colleague.

**Transferable principle:** a useful invitation can change the remedy; it does not merely soften an accusation.

**Bounded practice:** replace one motive label in the opening with a supplied observation. After the real conversation, notice whether the proposed agreement responds to the account actually heard. A polite exchange alone does not establish that the handoff problem was resolved.

## 2. GUIDE: help the colleague test a choice without choosing for them

**Primary card:** [GUIDE](../cards/13-develop.md), Chapter 13, pp. 194–211. Source anchor: Sample coaching conversation using the GUIDE model.

**Source note.** Webb presents a supplier dialogue based on a story told by Shellye Archambeau. The teaching dialogue moves from launch requirements and supplier uncertainty to alternative plans, then questions an either-or assumption. It ends with information-gathering actions and requested support. The excerpt does not establish that the proposed hybrid plan was implemented or succeeded; it is not a verbatim transcript.

### Situation and fit

**Hypothetical user:** “Help me coach a colleague who is preparing a supplier recommendation. Their goal is a reliable product launch with acceptable quality. They are weighing our current supplier, which delivered late twice, against a new supplier that hasn't yet demonstrated it can meet our requirements.”

GUIDE fits the request to help the colleague develop their own thinking. The goal and initial options are already supplied. Ask about a decisive gap or assumption rather than making them repeat these facts. If the user instead requests help comparing the suppliers directly, [ACORN](../cards/04-decide.md) may fit that task; do not require a coaching interview first.

### Weaker and better moves on the same facts

**Weaker:** “Don't you think splitting the order would be the best solution?” This disguises the leader's preferred answer as a question and assumes the split is feasible.

**Better original question for the planned conversation:** “Which assumption in each plan would need checking before you could recommend it?” Explain that the purpose is to expose missing evidence, not to make the colleague guess the leader's answer.

### Follow the information instead of reciting every GUIDE step

Each row is a possible next turn after hearing the preceding answer, not a list of questions to send at once. The stage labels explain the method to the host.

| What is available now | Relevant move | Original example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The goal, initial options and major uncertainty are already given. | Deepen the assumptions. | “Which assumption in each plan would need checking before you could recommend it?” |
| The colleague says they assumed all supply had to come from one partner. | Reopen Ideas; examine whether that constraint is real. | “Is a single supplier a confirmed requirement, or part of the current plan?” |
| It is an assumption and the colleague wants suggestions. | Offer an option transparently, then test it. | “One option to compare is a split arrangement. We would still need evidence about coordination, quality and capacity.” |
| The colleague identifies a candidate direction and a decisive evidence gap. | Enable the next step. | “What information would let you test this before committing, and what help would you need?” |

**Hypothetical correction branch:** the user clarifies that launch volume and date are fixed. Remove scope reduction or delay from the proposed options. Do not return to them later as though they remained available. A split arrangement remains only a candidate if it can satisfy the fixed requirements.

**Hypothetical missing-knowledge branch:** the colleague does not know how to assess supplier qualification and asks for instruction. Explain the relevant assessment or help identify qualified support. Endless discovery questions cannot supply missing expertise. Return to their recommendation once they have enough information to reason about it.

### Stop, hand off and practise

Stop when the colleague can describe a candidate direction, its critical assumption and the next evidence-gathering action. Establish an owner and timing only from actual agreement. The supplier decision, purchase and launch changes still belong to the appropriate people. A full decision matrix or a second complete framework is unnecessary unless the comparison calls for it.

**Example handoff:** “The launch requirements remain the criteria. A split arrangement is a candidate, not an approved order. Qualification and capacity are still unknown. Next, obtain the evidence needed to compare it; keep the fixed launch constraints visible.” No successful rollout or consent from Sales is inferred.

**Transferable principle:** support another person's judgment by making assumptions discussable and help explicit; the apparent two-option choice may itself be an assumption.

**Bounded practice:** take one preferred option and state one assumption plus the evidence that could overturn it. Notice whether the colleague can explain their recommendation and next step in their own words. Agreement with the leader is not, by itself, evidence of learning.

## 3. Support: reduce demands without quietly transferring overload

**Primary card:** [CALM](../cards/18-support.md), Chapter 18, pp. 274–287. Source anchor: Make things Manageable → Situation A: Managing overload by reallocating work.

**Source note.** Mariah Levin's account describes an experienced event organizer whose workload and travel had become difficult to sustain. They shared the work and prepared a junior colleague through shadowing before an event assignment. Levin reports a more distributed approach with less demanding travel. This is the leader's account in the book, not independently verified evidence for this skill.

### Situation and fit

**Hypothetical user:** “A colleague who leads our event preparation says the travel and regular workload are too much. They want less travel. I want to help, but I don't want to overload someone else.”

The colleague's account already identifies demands to discuss. CALM fits practical support; do not diagnose a health condition or require a second interview to obtain the same account. If the problem turns out to affect the whole team's working conditions, [Chapter 21](../cards/21-team-resilience.md) may become the primary method for that broader task.

### Weaker and better moves on the same facts

**Weaker:** “Teach them a reset exercise and give the travel to a junior colleague.” That leaves the demand unchanged and assumes another person's capacity and readiness.

**Better original opening:** “You've said the travel and regular workload are too much, and you want less travel. Which demand would make the biggest difference to reduce first?” Reflect their answer before proposing a change. If they have already identified that demand, move directly to feasible options.

### New information changes the next move

These are hypothetical branches, not facts to assume about the team:

| New information | Useful next move | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The user reports assignment authority; another colleague wants a bounded event role and has confirmed relevant readiness and available time. | Discuss a defined handoff, any preparation or shadowing needed, support and what work is removed from the overloaded colleague. [Fix-Flex](../cards/11-delegate.md) can support the handoff if that becomes the task. | Asking for supplied authority again, assuming all implementation is approved, or leaving the original owner responsible for doing everything. |
| A willing, prepared colleague has time, but the user explicitly lacks assignment authority. | Prepare a proposed adjustment and its workload implications for the known approving owner. | Directing reassignment as though the user could approve it. |
| The colleague's capacity is reported, but assignment authority is unknown. | Present reassignment as a conditional option; identify approval as the first implementation check. Preparation can proceed without a full role interview. | Treating missing authority evidence as either approval or a ban on proposing anything. |
| Nobody has spare capacity and the user cannot add resources. | Identify what could be reduced, deferred or stopped, and prepare the remaining trade-off for the authorized decision-maker. | Quietly redistributing the same impossible workload or guaranteeing a budget, leave approval or replacement. |
| The user does not know who can approve a scope or resource change. | Prepare the unresolved decision and check the applicable approval route with an available manager or process contact, if one exists. | Inventing an owner or stopping at “ask the decision-maker” when that is the missing information. |

The branches are alternatives, not instructions to add CALM, Fix-Flex and the 5 Cs to every answer. A person may decline to discuss private circumstances; work-related support can still be considered. Medical or urgent safety needs require appropriate human help, not a stronger coaching script.

### Decision handoff when reassignment is not ready

**Hypothetical continuation:** the user reports that nobody has spare capacity, extra resources need approval and they do not know the approving owner. A useful original decision brief could be:

> The colleague reports that travel and regular work are too much. No available recipient has been identified. The decision needed is which demand to reduce or whether to approve additional capacity. Options to assess are reducing event scope, deferring a commitment where timing allows, or requesting additional support; their acceptability is not yet established. The approval route is unknown. I can prepare the affected tasks and constraints, then check who can approve the trade-off before proposing an implementation.

This makes a decision ready for the appropriate person to consider; it does not make the decision for them. Do not invent a mandatory policy, an acceptable delay or a budget. If the user names an authorized owner, address the brief to that person. If authority and recipient capacity are later supplied, move to the scoped Fix-Flex handoff instead of repeating the approval question.

### Stop, hand off and practise

Stop when there is a feasible proposed adjustment or a clearly stated capacity decision for the relevant owner. If reassignment is viable, check both people's workload and preparation before treating the plan as ready. A supportive conversation need not establish a personal diagnosis to reach this point.

**Example handoff:** “Reducing travel is the reported need. Reassignment is conditional on verified capacity, preparation and approval. If those are unavailable, take a scope or resource trade-off to the decision-maker. The change has not yet been implemented.” The assistant has not reassigned work or created a check-in.

**Transferable principle:** practical support changes the demands or resources; it must remain workable for the people receiving the work too.

**Bounded practice:** map one task proposed for removal, its remaining owner and any preparation required. After an actual change, check whether it freed usable time without overloading the recipient. A grateful reply is not evidence that capacity improved.

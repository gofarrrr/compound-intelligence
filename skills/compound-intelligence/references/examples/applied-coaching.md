# Applied coaching demonstrations

These are original hypothetical examples, not results of live evaluation.

## 1. Missed handoffs: facts before motives

**User:** “Alex missed the Tuesday and Thursday handoffs. The receiving team had to postpone testing. I think Alex doesn’t care, and I need to address it.”

**Example response:**

Use **Factual Feedback** here: you have two specific observations and a work impact, but “doesn’t care” is still an interpretation. The first conversation should clarify what happened, not prove that motive. *(Webb, Chapter 12, pp. 180–193.)*

A possible opening is: “I want us to make the handoffs reliable. The Tuesday and Thursday handoffs did not happen, and the receiving team postponed testing. What happened from your side?” Then listen before agreeing on the change and support needed.

The coaching move is to separate **observation → implication → invitation** within the fuller developmental-feedback sequence. It lets you be direct about the work without claiming access to Alex’s intentions. Before the meeting, replace one label in your notes with something another observer could have seen. Notice whether the conversation produces both a better explanation and a concrete next agreement. If the account reveals overload or missing resources, address those conditions rather than simply repeating the expectation.

**Why this route:** a specific behavior and impact support Chapter 12. Chapter 18 becomes relevant if the colleague reveals a struggle needing support; Chapter 20 is not justified by this initial account.

## 2. Change that is not taking hold: inspect the system

**User:** “We ask staff to log support cases immediately, but their target rewards the number of calls completed. Logging takes time. Reminders have not helped.”

**Example response:**

Start with **CASE**, particularly **Appealing** and **Easy**. Your account already identifies a possible mismatch between the requested behavior and the rewarded behavior, plus a time cost. Another explanation of why logging matters may not remove either obstacle. *(Webb, Chapter 9, pp. 136–152.)*

Check how the target is actually applied and observe one real logging step. Then propose a bounded change: recognize complete case handling in the target or remove verified logging friction, with the relevant owner’s approval. Do not announce a new incentive or alter the system without that authority.

The lesson is to make the desired behavior practical and coherently rewarded, not just more strongly requested. In the next review, compare the actual behavior under the revised conditions. A reminder being read is not evidence that the workflow changed.

**Why this route:** the goal is already expressed; the immediate bottleneck is behavior under conflicting incentives, not a lack of inspirational messaging.

## 3. “More resilience” is the wrong first request

**User:** “The team is covering two vacant roles. Deadlines have not moved, and everyone is working late. How do I teach them to be more resilient?”

**Example response:**

The first issue to examine is **Capacity** in the **5 Cs of Team Resilience**, not whether people are trying hard enough to cope. Your facts describe fewer people carrying unchanged work. *(Webb, Chapter 21, pp. 319–331.)*

Make the workload trade-off explicit: what must continue, what can be deferred or stopped, and what needs additional resources or a changed deadline? Prepare that decision for the person who has authority to make it. In the meantime, identify a practical boundary that does not merely transfer the late work to someone less able to refuse it.

The coaching lesson is that healthy resilience includes the conditions of work. Purpose and recovery habits may help, but they do not create missing capacity. Try one genuine stop, defer, or resource decision and check whether usable time improves. Do not measure success by attendance at a wellbeing session while the load stays unchanged.

**Why this route:** Chapter 15 could support a person in an acute moment, but it would not resolve the team-wide conditions presented here.

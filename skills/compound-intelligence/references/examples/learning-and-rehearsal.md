# Learning and rehearsal demonstrations

Original hypothetical examples; not book dialogue or evidence of live model performance.

## Learn: a genuine invitation in feedback

**Source:** Factual Feedback, Chapter 12; Show and Explain, Chapter 13.

**Objective:** distinguish a real question from a criticism with a question mark.

**Less useful:** “Why are you always so careless?” This contains a character judgment, a sweeping claim, and a presumed cause.

**Improved, for a defined hypothetical case:** “The report went to the client without the agreed cost check. That left an unverified number in the report. What happened before it was sent?” The observation and impact are explicit, while the explanation remains open. These facts belong only to this hypothetical exercise.

**Explain the move:** an invitation should allow information that changes your understanding. It is not a device for making the other person confess to your interpretation.

**Exercise:** rewrite “Why do you keep ignoring the process?” You do not yet have a specific incident. A good response first asks for the actual observation, or uses a clearly marked placeholder rather than inventing one.

**Self-check:** Does the revised line describe a supplied behavior? Does it avoid guessing the motive? Could the answer genuinely change the plan?

**Transfer:** if the issue turns out to be a workload conflict rather than disregard of the process, address the workload or priority conflict. Repeating the same feedback louder is not the only next move.

## Rehearse: a delegation boundary

**Known hypothetical facts:** a colleague owns a presentation; the client’s submission date is fixed; the colleague can choose its structure; a check-in is already agreed. No other client requirements are assumed.

**Source:** Fix-Flex, Chapter 11; the role-play protocol is package design.

**Simulation notice:** the response below is a plausible practice turn, not a prediction about a real person.

**Leader’s first attempt:** “You can do it your way, but use the structure I sent.”

**Practice counterpart:** “Which part am I actually choosing?”

**Debrief:** the first sentence offers autonomy and then withdraws it. Separate a real fixed boundary from a preference.

**Retry:** “The client’s submission date is fixed. The structure is yours to propose. At our agreed check-in, let’s examine whether your approach serves the outcome and where you need support.”

**What changed:** the fixed constraint is explicit, the choice is real, and the check-in supports rather than disguises control. A smoother simulated response would not prove the real colleague will react the same way.

# 06 · How to Handle a Disagreement Well

**Framework:** Common Ground Technique

**Source:** Webb, *Leadership Intelligence*, Chapter 6, printed pp. 85–98. **Pillar:** Deliberate Wisdom.

The named framework and its central moves are source-derived. The routing boundaries, output format, original coaching question, practice check, and safety constraints are implementation adaptations, not quotations or independently validated interventions.

## Select this when
Work through a specific disagreement, make a constructive challenge, or prepare a principled negotiation.

## Route elsewhere when
Use ci-stakeholder for a recurring relationship pattern. Harassment, threats, or retaliation are not ordinary disagreements to be solved by finding common ground.

## Coaching explanation
Webb uses genuine understanding to reduce reflexive opposition and reveal the precise difference. The goal is a better joint understanding or next step, not agreement at any cost.

## Apply the relevant moves

### Clarify the positions
Establish what each person is actually proposing when this is not yet clear. Separate observed statements from inferred motives.

### Represent their view fairly
Describe the strongest reasonable version that is supported by their stated position. Check your understanding instead of inventing their intentions.

### Find authentic agreement
Identify what is genuinely shared: a goal, a fact, a constraint, or part of an approach. Do not invent agreement as a persuasion tactic.

### Locate the difference
State the exact point of disagreement and the assumptions or constraints behind it. Consider whether different conditions could make both views reasonable; do not force equivalence when evidence contradicts one claim.

### Choose a way forward
Agree a test, a combined approach, a decision process, or a respectful boundary. An acknowledged unresolved difference can be an honest result.

## Supporting techniques and spotlights
Challenging Constructively, p. 95: Tell Me More is an invitation to understand, not a disguised criticism. Negotiating Effectively, pp. 96–98: clarify priorities, compromises, walk-away conditions, and the best alternative to an agreement; consider the counterpart’s needs as hypotheses; broaden possible terms and make agreements explicit. Do not bluff facts or manufacture social pressure.

## Useful output
A fair statement of each view, authentic common ground, the precise unresolved difference, and a proposed test, agreement, or decision path.

## Original coaching question
What part of their position could you explain in a way they would recognize as fair?

## Small practice
Restate the other view and ask for correction before offering your challenge.

## Observe and adjust
You can name the actual disagreement without attacking the person or pretending the difference has disappeared.

## Common misuse
Do not confuse being generous about a view with abandoning standards, evidence, or safety.

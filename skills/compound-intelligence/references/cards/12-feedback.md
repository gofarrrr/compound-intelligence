# 12 · How to Give Feedback That Drives Action

**Framework:** Factual Feedback

**Source:** Webb, *Leadership Intelligence*, Chapter 12, printed pp. 180–193. **Pillar:** Intrinsic Inspiration.

The named framework and its central moves are source-derived. The routing boundaries, output format, original coaching question, practice check, and safety constraints are implementation adaptations, not quotations or independently validated interventions.

## Select this when
Recognize a specific contribution or discuss an observed behavior and its impact in a way that supports action.

## Route elsewhere when
Use ci-support when someone may be struggling and needs an initial supportive conversation. Use ci-develop for capability-building rather than a specific feedback event.

## Coaching explanation
Webb focuses feedback on observable behavior and consequences rather than character judgments. An invitation to understand the situation makes improvement more collaborative and less dependent on guessed motives.

## Apply the relevant moves

### Choose the form
For praise, use Observation → Implication: name what happened and why it mattered. For developmental feedback, use the five steps below; do not sandwich the issue between invented compliments.

### Intention
State the genuine constructive purpose. Do not use caring language to conceal punishment or a decision already made.

### Observation
Describe the concrete behavior, event, or work product. Use supplied examples and dates only. Label secondhand reports as reports; avoid personality labels, always/never claims, and invented incidents.

### Implication
Explain the supported effect on the work or others. Distinguish observed impact from a risk or interpretation.

### Invitation
Ask for the colleague’s perspective and listen before deciding why it happened. The question must allow an answer other than the leader’s preferred explanation.

### Collaboration
Agree the practical change, support, and a proportionate follow-up. Keep developmental feedback appropriately private and invite feedback on the leader’s contribution too.

## Useful output
A feedback scaffold using only supported facts, a genuine invitation, and a proposed next-step agreement. Mark missing examples explicitly rather than filling them in.

## Original coaching question
What exactly happened that another observer could have noticed?

## Small practice
Replace one character judgment with an observation and an implication, then pause for the other person’s account.

## Observe and adjust
The colleague can understand what behavior is being discussed and can contribute to a concrete improvement plan.

## Common misuse
Do not assume silence means agreement or use a warm tone to soften an accusation that lacks evidence.

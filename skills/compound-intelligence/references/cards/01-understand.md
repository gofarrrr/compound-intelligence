# 01 · How to Better Understand a Complex Situation

**Framework:** 360-Degree Vision

**Source:** Webb, *Leadership Intelligence*, Chapter 1, printed pp. 15–29. **Pillar:** Deliberate Wisdom.

The named framework and its central moves are source-derived. The routing boundaries, output format, original coaching question, practice check, and safety constraints are implementation adaptations, not quotations or independently validated interventions.

## Select this when
Understand a confusing situation, conflicting accounts, or an uncertain cause before choosing a solution.

## Route elsewhere when
A well-understood choice between options belongs in ci-decide. A known problem with too few ideas belongs in ci-ideate.

## Coaching explanation
Webb describes how selective attention and familiar interpretations can narrow what a leader notices. Widen the evidence before making the explanation more confident.

## Apply the relevant moves

### See Deeper
Separate direct observations, reported accounts, interpretations, and unknowns. Ask non-leading questions and request a concrete example. Use Go and See to examine the actual work when access is authorized. Use Brainwriting to collect independent views before group discussion.

### See Differently
Seek a relevant perspective missing from the current account: a frontline colleague, another function, a customer, or a genuine dissenting voice. A simulated adviser can generate questions, but cannot supply testimony or establish what another person thinks.

### See Across
Compare periods, teams, levels, and inside/outside views. Look for relevant base rates and situations where the problem does not occur. Do not mistake a convenient comparison for a valid one.

### See the System
Sketch the interacting conditions and possible feedback loops. Label each causal arrow as supported or hypothesized. Identify a leverage point and the observation that would test it; a busy-looking map does not prove causality.

## Useful output
A short situation map: known facts, two or more plausible explanations when the evidence permits, the most important unknown, and the next discriminating observation.

## Original coaching question
What have you directly observed, and what explanation have you added to it?

## Small practice
Before your next conclusion, write one alternative explanation and one observation that would distinguish it from your current view.

## Observe and adjust
You can explain why the next information-gathering step could change the decision, rather than merely confirm your first story.

## Common misuse
Do not turn inquiry into endless research, or treat a causal diagram as established evidence.
